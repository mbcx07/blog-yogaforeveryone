The Unseen Work: Mastering Bandhas and Core Engagement in Advanced Poses

2026-06-03 · Advanced Poses · Tags: The, advanced poses

When you watch an experienced yogi float effortlessly into a handstand or hold a deep backbend with serene composure, it’s easy to assume the secret lies in raw flexibility or brute strength. But ask any advanced practitioner, and they’ll tell you the real magic happens beneath the surface—in the subtle, invisible work of the bandhas (energy locks) and deep core engagement. These internal actions are the scaffolding that transforms a shaky, unsustainable pose into one that feels light, stable, and almost gravity-defying. Whether you’re working toward your first forearm stand or refining a transition from Crow to Tripod Headstand, understanding and cultivating these inner locks will elevate your practice from mere contortion to embodied mastery. In this article, we’ll explore the three primary bandhas, how they interact with your core, and practical ways to integrate them into your advanced asana practice.

Understanding the Three Bandhas: Your Internal Support System

The word bandha translates to “lock” or “bind,” and in yoga, these are subtle muscular contractions that direct energy flow and create structural integrity. There are three main bandhas, each with a distinct location and function:

  • Mula Bandha (Root Lock): Located at the pelvic floor, between the pubic bone and tailbone. Engaging Mula Bandha involves gently lifting the perineum upward, as if you’re stopping the flow of urine. This lock stabilizes the pelvis, supports the lower back, and creates a foundation for inversions and arm balances.
  • Uddiyana Bandha (Upward Abdominal Lock): Found in the lower abdomen, just below the navel. After a full exhalation, you draw the belly inward and upward toward the spine. This lock tones the core, massages the internal organs, and provides lift in poses like Handstand and Bakasana (Crow Pose).
  • Jalandhara Bandha (Throat Lock): Activated by lowering the chin toward the chest while lifting the sternum. This lock regulates the flow of prana to the head and is often used in pranayama and inversions like Shoulderstand. It also protects the cervical spine during deep forward folds.

While each bandha can be practiced in isolation, advanced poses require them to work in harmony. Think of Mula Bandha as the anchor, Uddiyana Bandha as the elevator, and Jalandhara Bandha as the stabilizer. Together, they create a unified lift that lightens your body and sharpens your awareness.

The Role of Core Engagement: Beyond Crunches and Planks

In the context of advanced yoga, “core” is not just your six-pack muscles. It’s a 360-degree cylinder of support that includes the deep transverse abdominis, the obliques, the multifidus along the spine, and the pelvic floor. When you engage your core properly, you’re not just bracing for a pose—you’re creating a dynamic, responsive container that allows your limbs to move freely while your torso remains stable.

One common misconception is that core engagement means sucking in your belly. In reality, it’s a subtle drawing in and up, similar to the action of Uddiyana Bandha but with a softer, more continuous quality. To feel this, try lying on your back with knees bent. Place your fingertips on your lower belly. Inhale, then exhale fully. On the exhale, gently draw your navel toward your spine without hardening your rectus abdominis. You should feel a slight lift in the pelvic floor as well—that’s Mula Bandha waking up. This coordinated action is the foundation for every advanced pose.

In arm balances, a strong core prevents you from collapsing into your shoulders. In backbends, it protects the lumbar spine by distributing the curve evenly. In inversions, it creates the lightness needed to float up. Without this internal engagement, you’re relying on superficial muscles and joint compression—a recipe for injury and frustration.

Integrating Bandhas into Advanced Poses: Practical Techniques

Now that you understand the theory, let’s apply it. Here are three advanced poses where bandha awareness can transform your experience:

Bakasana (Crow Pose): Before lifting your feet, engage Mula Bandha by gently lifting the pelvic floor. Then, as you exhale, draw your lower belly in (Uddiyana Bandha). This creates a hollowing effect that lifts your hips higher and shifts your weight forward. Instead of feeling like you’re balancing on your hands, you’ll feel buoyant—as if your core is a magnet pulling your knees toward your chest. Hold for five breaths, maintaining the locks throughout.

Pincha Mayurasana (Forearm Stand): Start in Dolphin Pose. Before kicking up, engage all three bandhas: Mula, Uddiyana, and a soft Jalandhara (chin slightly tucked). As you lift one leg, keep the bandhas active. The tendency is to lose core engagement when you’re upside down, but maintaining Uddiyana Bandha prevents the lower back from arching excessively. Imagine your torso is a hollow tube, and the bandhas are the suction that keeps everything light. Practice against a wall initially, focusing on holding the bandhas for three to five breaths before lowering.

Urdhva Dhanurasana (Wheel Pose): Lie on your back with knees bent. Press into your feet and hands, but before lifting, engage Mula Bandha. As you push up, draw your lower belly in (Uddiyana) to prevent the ribs from flaring. The bandhas here act as a corset, protecting your lower back and allowing the front body to open safely. Many students find that activating Mula Bandha in backbends creates a surprising sense of stability in the sacrum.

Remember: bandhas are not a death grip. They are a subtle, continuous engagement that you can maintain while breathing. If you find yourself holding your breath, you’re gripping too hard. Back off slightly and find the sweet spot between effort and ease.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Even experienced practitioners can fall into traps when working with bandhas. Here are the most common pitfalls and how to correct them:

  • Over-gripping the pelvic floor: Mula Bandha should feel like a gentle lift, not a clench. If you’re squeezing your glutes or inner thighs, you’ve gone too far. Practice in a seated position: inhale, exhale, and on the exhale, lift the pelvic floor just 10–20%. That’s enough.
  • Holding your breath: Uddiyana Bandha is often taught after a full exhalation, but in dynamic poses, you need to breathe while maintaining the lock. Practice by taking a normal inhale, then on the exhale, draw the belly in and hold that engagement as you inhale again. It takes practice, but eventually the bandha becomes a background hum.
  • Forgetting Jalandhara Bandha in inversions: When you’re upside down, the natural tendency is to let the head drop back, compressing the neck. A soft Jalandhara Bandha (chin slightly tucked, back of neck long) protects the cervical spine and keeps the energy flowing upward. In Headstand, for example, the crown of the head should be on the floor, not the forehead, and the chin should be slightly tucked.
  • Relying on bandhas to compensate for poor alignment: Bandhas are not a substitute for proper skeletal alignment. If your hips are misaligned in a standing pose, no amount of Mula Bandha will fix it. Always prioritize alignment first, then layer in the bandhas.

To check yourself, practice in front of a mirror or record a video. Notice if your belly is bulging or your pelvic floor is dropping. The bandhas should create a subtle inward lift, not a visible contraction.

A Practice Sequence to Explore Bandhas and Core Engagement

To build your awareness, try this short sequence at home. Move slowly, holding each pose for five breaths, and focus on the bandhas rather than depth or duration.

1. Seated Bandha Awareness (Sukhasana): Sit cross-legged. Close your eyes. Inhale, exhale fully. On the exhale, engage Mula Bandha (lift pelvic floor) and Uddiyana Bandha (draw navel in). Hold the locks for a few seconds, then release. Repeat five times. This trains the neuromuscular connection.

2. Cat-Cow with Bandhas: Come to all fours. Inhale, drop the belly (Cow), but keep Mula Bandha engaged. Exhale, round the spine (Cat), and draw the navel strongly in (Uddiyana). Feel how the bandhas deepen the spinal wave.

3. Plank to Chaturanga: From Plank, engage Mula and Uddiyana. As you lower into Chaturanga, maintain the locks—this prevents the lower back from sagging. Hold Chaturanga for one breath, then press back to Plank. Repeat three times.

4. Dolphin Pose with Bandhas: From Dolphin, walk your feet closer to your elbows. Engage all three bandhas. Hold for five breaths, feeling the lightness in your shoulders. This prepares you for Forearm Stand.

5. Supported Bridge Pose (Setu Bandhasana): Lie on your back, knees bent, feet hip-width apart. Press into your feet and lift your hips. Engage Mula Bandha and draw the lower belly in. Hold for five breaths, then lower. Notice how the bandhas create a sense of lift without straining the glutes.

Finish with a few minutes in Savasana, observing the subtle sensations in your pelvic floor and abdomen. Over time, this awareness will become second nature, and your advanced poses will feel more effortless.

Conclusion

The bandhas are often called the “secret” of advanced yoga, but they’re not meant to be hidden—they’re meant to be felt. By cultivating Mula, Uddiyana, and Jalandhara Bandha, along with a deep, 360-degree core engagement, you unlock a new dimension of stability, lightness, and control. These internal actions don’t just improve your asanas; they teach you to work with your energy, to find power without force, and to move with grace even in the most challenging shapes. As you continue your practice, let the unseen work become your foundation. The poses will follow.
